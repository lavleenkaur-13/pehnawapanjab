The History of Phulkari: 500 Years of Punjabi Embroidery

Published 12 May 2026

Phulkari, literally translating to "flower work", is one of the most celebrated textile traditions of Punjab. For over 500 years, the women of Punjab have been creating these intricate hand-embroidered pieces — not as commercial products, but as deeply personal gifts of love, prayer and tradition.

The Origins: Punjab, 15th Century

The earliest references to phulkari appear in Punjabi folklore, particularly in the romantic epic of Heer-Ranjha, written by Sufi poet Waris Shah in 1766. The verses describe Heer's trousseau, complete with phulkari embroidered cloths — suggesting that by the mid-1700s, phulkari was already a well-established part of Punjabi domestic life.

Historians trace the craft even further back. Some scholars connect phulkari's origins to Central Asian embroidery traditions that travelled to Punjab through trade routes during the 15th century. Others argue the craft developed independently in rural Punjab, evolving from simpler darning techniques used to repair and decorate handwoven cotton.

How Phulkari Was Made (and Still Is)

Authentic phulkari follows a remarkably specific process that has barely changed in five centuries:

  1. The base fabric is khaddar — coarse, handwoven cotton cloth, traditionally produced by the same Punjabi village that did the embroidery.
  2. The thread is pat — untwisted floss silk in vibrant colours, sourced historically from Kashmir, Afghanistan and Bengal.
  3. The stitch is darning stitch — worked entirely on the reverse side of the fabric, with the design showing through to the front. This is what gives phulkari its distinctive raised, almost three-dimensional texture.
  4. The maker was traditionally a woman of the family — a mother, grandmother or aunt — embroidering the piece over months or years for a daughter's wedding.

Types of Phulkari: Bagh, Chope and Beyond

Not all phulkari is the same. Over the centuries, distinct styles emerged across different regions of Punjab:

  • Bagh (literally "garden") — the most prestigious form, where embroidery covers the fabric so completely that the base cloth is no longer visible. A bagh phulkari could take 2-3 years to complete and was considered a family heirloom.
  • Chope — embroidered on red khaddar, traditionally gifted by the maternal grandmother at a granddaughter's wedding. Features geometric patterns in golden yellow silk.
  • Sainchi — pictorial phulkari from the Bhatinda region, depicting scenes of village life, animals, and the everyday world of Punjab.
  • Thirma — embroidered on white khaddar, traditionally for elderly women. The white base symbolized purity and the wisdom of age.

The Cultural Significance

In traditional Punjabi culture, phulkari was never just a piece of clothing. It was a marker of life's most important moments. A daughter's birth was celebrated with the start of a new phulkari. Her wedding trousseau wasn't complete without multiple phulkaris embroidered by her grandmother, mother and aunts. After her marriage, the phulkaris were stored carefully, brought out only for major life events.

The motifs themselves carried meaning. Wheat sheaves represented prosperity. Geometric stars symbolized protection. Floral patterns were prayers for happiness. Every phulkari, in this sense, was both a piece of art and a piece of family history.

Phulkari Today: The Revival

The partition of Punjab in 1947 was nearly catastrophic for phulkari. Entire communities of practitioners were displaced; the knowledge of specific regional styles was lost as families scattered. By the 1980s, authentic hand-embroidered phulkari was becoming rare, replaced by cheap machine-made imitations.

The revival began in the 2000s, led by craft revival organisations and a new generation of Punjabi designers who refused to let the tradition die. Today, authentic phulkari is increasingly recognized as a heritage craft worth preserving — and worth paying for. UNESCO has included phulkari in discussions of intangible cultural heritage, and the Geographical Indication (GI) tag for Phulkari from Punjab was granted in 2010.

How to Spot Authentic Phulkari

If you're shopping for genuine handcrafted phulkari, here's what to look for:

  • Examine the reverse side. Authentic hand-embroidered phulkari uses darning stitch, so the back will show the design just as prominently as the front. Machine-made imitations have a clear "front" and a messy "back".
  • Look for irregularities. Hand-embroidery has small variations — slight differences in stitch length, occasional colour bleeding, the human imprint. Perfectly uniform stitches indicate machine work.
  • Feel the thread. Real pat silk is untwisted and has a distinctive softness. Polyester imitations feel slick and shiny.
  • Ask about the artisan. Authentic sellers can tell you where the piece was made, by whom, and roughly how long it took. If the seller can't answer these questions, walk away.
